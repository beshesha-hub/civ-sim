This report documents three validation analyses applied to civ-sim's 8-country calibration set: uncertainty quantification across 100 stochastic seeds, one-at-a-time sensitivity sweeps on 8 input parameters, and leave-one-out cross-validation testing whether model behavior generalizes beyond initial-state tuning.

The results are mixed. Wealth concentration and polarization are robustly determined by configuration; social trust and corruption are noise-dominated in several countries. The model's mechanisms generalize well for Nordic/egalitarian configurations but struggle with hybrid regimes (China, Singapore, Saudi Arabia). Hindcasting against four historical transitions (37% overall) revealed that polarization-driven instability is well-captured (Chile at 71%) but developmental state dynamics (South Korea at 9%) and system collapse (Russia at 36%) are structural gaps. Education quality and governance hierarchy are the dominant sensitivity levers.

Honest assessment: Civ-sim performs well as a qualitative exploration tool for democratic/authoritarian extremes and polarization dynamics. It cannot reproduce developmental state trajectories (state-directed capacity building despite autocracy) or rapid institutional collapse. The 42% overfit rate in cross-validation and 37% hindcast pass rate mean the model is best positioned as an exploration tool for "what-if" directional analysis, not a predictive instrument.

Key findings: Wealth concentration is the most robust output — robust in all 8 countries, because the multiplicative wealth dynamics and Pareto-tail mechanics are structurally stable. Polarization is also highly robust. Social trust and corruption are the least robust, particularly for mid-range configurations where the bistable trust–corruption attractor can tip either way depending on stochastic event timing.

Target coverage at 46% means that fewer than half the real-world benchmark values fall within the model's 80% confidence interval (p10–p90). The main systematic biases are: trust too high for Denmark (+17), trust too low for Russia (−25) and Saudi (−39), wealth concentration too high across authoritarian configurations, and corruption floor too low for developed democracies (the model drives corruption to 0 where real-world levels are 4–27).

Pattern Analysis

Where the model generalizes: Democratic/egalitarian configurations (Denmark, USA) and extreme inequality configurations (Nigeria corruption, Russia corruption/wealth, Brazil wealth). These represent the stable attractors of the bistable trust–corruption system — the model's mechanisms naturally produce the right qualitative behavior at both extremes.

Where the model overfits: Hybrid regimes (China, Singapore, Saudi Arabia) and corruption for democracies (USA, Denmark, Brazil). The model lacks mechanisms for: (a) state-directed capitalism that constrains wealth concentration despite autocracy (China, Singapore), (b) meritocratic autocracy that suppresses corruption without democratic accountability (Singapore), and (c) moderate corruption equilibria in developed democracies (the floor problem).

Structural gap — corruption floor: The model drives corruption to 0 for any configuration with high education quality and moderate-to-low hierarchy. Real-world democracies sustain corruption levels of 4–27 (CPI-derived). This is the single most consistent bias across the model.

Trajectory Analysis

Chile (71% — best performer): The model correctly reproduces the instability–recovery arc. High initial polarization (85) drives stability below 40 by turn 5, matching the 1973 coup period. Stability then recovers to 65 by turn 30, matching Chile's democratic consolidation. Institutional quality rises to 57. The model misses the magnitude of wealth concentration increase under Pinochet (simulated 32 vs. target 50+) because the simulation lacks a mechanism for authoritarian wealth extraction.

Rwanda (44%): The model produces the initial collapse (stability drops to 17 by turn 5), matching the genocide period. Wellbeing is above 15 by turn 15 (recovery underway). However, state capacity falls to 7 instead of rising — the model can't produce Rwanda's remarkable capacity recovery under developmental autocracy. Gender equity stays at 0 instead of rising to 70+ (the model lacks a mechanism for deliberate equity policy by an autocratic regime).

Russia (36%): Military power (84) and institutional lock-in (87) are correctly high at turn 30. But the model completely fails to produce the Soviet collapse: stability stays at 55–70 (should crash), wealth concentration stays at 20–21 (should explode to 80+), corruption stays near 0 (should be 60+). The planned economy + high education + high state capacity configuration is too stable — the model lacks mechanisms for legitimacy crisis and rapid institutional disintegration.

South Korea (9% — worst performer): Near-complete failure. State capacity declines from 45 to 8 when it should rise to 80+. Wellbeing declines from 47 to 14 when it should rise to 60+. The model's equation hierarchy → low trust → low capacity dominates, but real developmental states (Korea, Taiwan, Singapore) build capacity through directed hierarchy — a mechanism the model lacks entirely.

Structural gaps revealed by hindcasting:

1. No developmental state mechanism: The model treats hierarchy as universally corrosive to state capacity. Real developmental states use hierarchy to build capacity rapidly. This affects Korea, Rwanda, Singapore, and China.

2. No system collapse mechanism: High lock-in + high state capacity configurations can't produce rapid institutional disintegration. The Soviet collapse is the paradigmatic case.

3. No authoritarian wealth extraction: The model doesn't distinguish between extractive autocracy (wealth concentration rises) and developmental autocracy (wealth concentration is managed).

4. No deliberate equity policy: Gender equity and similar metrics lack a mechanism for top-down policy intervention, making Rwanda's equity revolution invisible.

5. Implications for Public Use

What civ-sim can credibly claim

  • Qualitative directional predictions: higher hierarchy and lower education produce worse outcomes across trust, stability, and state capacity
  • Robust wealth concentration dynamics: the Pareto-tail mechanics produce stable, credible inequality trajectories
  • Comparative analysis between regime types at the extremes (Nordic-style vs. kleptocratic)
  • Exploration of "what-if" scenarios for policy direction, not magnitude

What civ-sim cannot credibly claim

  • Point predictions for any specific country (46% target coverage is below standard)
  • Accurate modeling of hybrid regimes (state capitalism, developmental autocracy)
  • Reliable corruption dynamics (floor problem + high stochastic variance)
  • Stability or trust predictions for mid-range configurations (noise-competitive regime)

Recommended framing for public release

"An integrative exploration tool that models interactions between governance, economics, culture, and institutions. Grounded in published research on wealth dynamics, institutional quality, and social trust. Validated for qualitative directional analysis; not calibrated for country-specific point predictions. Validation report available."

6. Methodology

Uncertainty quantification: 100 independent runs per scenario using seeds 1007, 2007, ..., 100007. Distributions computed on final-turn values. Coefficient of variation (CV) = stddev/|mean| × 100. Robustness thresholds: Robust < 15%, Moderate 15–30%, Noisy > 30%. Target coverage tests whether the real-world benchmark falls within the p10–p90 interval.

Sensitivity analysis: One-at-a-time sweeps on 8 parameters using USA scenario at seed 1001. Each parameter varied across 6–9 values spanning its plausible range. Elasticity = (Δoutput/baseline_output) / (Δinput/baseline_input) computed from the min-to-max range. Single-seed limitation: results reflect structural sensitivity but may miss stochastic interaction effects.

Cross-validation: Leave-one-out on initial state overrides. Each country run with full configuration (initialState + config) and stripped (config only, initialState = {}). Five seeds per run [1001, 2002, 3003, 4004, 5005]. Generalization threshold: stripped-mode error from target ≤ 15 points. This tests whether the model's mechanisms produce correct behavior from configuration alone, or whether manual initial-state tuning is load-bearing.

Hindcasting: Four historical transitions (South Korea 1960–2010, Chile 1970–2000, Russia 1985–2015, Rwanda 1990–2020) run from documented starting conditions with 20 seeds each. Historical initial conditions derived from V-Dem v14, Polity5, Maddison Project GDP data, World Bank WDI, and academic literature. Two evaluation modes: standard expectations (trend/threshold tests as used in calibration) and waypoint checks (specific metric thresholds at specific turns corresponding to known historical dates). Waypoint checks use mean across seeds, reported with standard deviation and seed pass rate.

Targets: Social trust from World Values Survey (2017–2022), wealth concentration from World Bank Gini × estimated top-decile share, corruption from Transparency International CPI (inverted to 0–100 scale where 100 = most corrupt).

Reproducibility: Analysis code: js/validation_suite.js (UQ, sensitivity, cross-validation) and js/hindcast_runner.js + js/hindcast_scenarios.js (hindcasting). Raw JSON results in validation_results/. Run node js/validation_suite.js all --seeds=100 and node js/hindcast_runner.js --seeds=20 to reproduce.
